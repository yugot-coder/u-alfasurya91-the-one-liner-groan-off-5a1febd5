# Candidate dad jokes — The One-Liner Groan-Off

Gate: one line, 1–140 characters, UTF-8, not only whitespace. All ten
below pass the gate. Judge criteria, most important first: (1) a one-line
dad joke, a pun or twist a dad would tell out loud; (2) punchline lands
fast and clean, setup and turn inside the single line; (3) fresh, not one
everyone has heard a hundred times; (4) tight and rhythmic, easy to say
out loud; (5) the groan is earned.

## Candidates

1. **"I keep a hammer in the fridge so I can nail the cold cuts."** (58 chars)
   — SELECTED. Double pun in the last four words: *nail* (what a hammer
   does) + *cold cuts* (the deli meat that lives in the fridge). The setup
   is one odd, funny image — a hammer in the fridge — and the punchline
   needs no explaining. Fresh: this exact pairing is not a recycled
   classic. Tight, rhythmic, and it is a dinner-table joke in the most
   literal sense: it is about what is in the fridge at dinner.
2. "My son asked why the car was in the oven. I told him I wanted a hot rod." (72 chars)
   — Strong runner-up. Kid-question format is pure dad voice, and *hot
   rod* lands on the last two words. Slightly weaker than #1: the hot-rod
   pun is a known pairing, so it loses a step on freshness.
3. "I put my watch in the blender. Now it is just a matter of time." (63 chars)
   — Very tight, punchline on the last words. But *matter of time* is a
   familiar idiom doing the work, and the blender-watch image is a
   one-note gag; less surprising than #1.
4. "My sourdough starter left me a note. It said it needed space to rise." (69 chars)
   — Fresh, modern-dad image (every dad has a sourdough phase) and *rise*
   does double duty. Weaker: the punchline is gentle, more smile than
   groan, and "needed space" is a therapy cliché.
5. "My daughter asked why the pillow was on the roof. I said it wanted to sleep on it." (82 chars)
   — Cute image, *sleep on it* lands cleanly. But the idiom is doing all
   the work and the setup (pillow on a roof) is random rather than earned.
6. "I told my son the vacuum cleaner quit. He asked why. I said it was just collecting dust." (88 chars)
   — *Collecting dust* = doing nothing is a neat double meaning. Weaker:
   three beats for one pun, and the middle beat ("He asked why") is dead
   air the judge's "no wasted words" criterion punishes.
7. "My wife asked why I talk to the lawn. I said I am trying to get to the root of our problems." (92 chars)
   — *Root* pun fits the lawn, and the setup is a real dad behaviour.
   Weaker: *root of our problems* is a stock phrase, so the twist feels
   borrowed rather than invented.
8. "My son asked why I brought a pencil to the restaurant. I said in case I had to draw the check." (94 chars)
   — *Draw the check* is a clean pun and the restaurant setting is
   dinner-table adjacent. Weaker: 94 characters for a single idiom pun,
   and the setup asks the listener to picture a pencil at dinner.
9. "My kid asked why the boat was in the garage. I said it is just going through a dry spell." (89 chars)
   — *Dry spell* for a boat on land is a good mismatch image. Weaker:
   the pun is a common idiom and the joke explains itself a beat too
   slowly.
10. "I asked the keyboard why it was so stressed. It said too many keys and no escape." (81 chars)
    — *Escape* key pun is clever and tech-dad. Weaker: it is a riddle
    ("why is X stressed?") rather than a dad telling a joke out loud,
    and the escape-key gag is a well-worn trope.

## Why #1 wins

It is the only candidate where the setup and the punchline are the same
image: a hammer belongs with nails, cold cuts belong in the fridge, and
the sentence puts them together so the pun fires twice at once. Nothing
is wasted (58 characters), nothing needs explaining, and no other entry
in the field is likely to have written this exact joke — the freshness
criterion is where most of the field will lose, and #1 is built for it.
